1.The whole machine on one page

The full system. Everything left of the model runs once on your Mac; the loop runs 590 times on Colab; the eval box runs before training (zero-shot baseline) and after (verdict).

In one paragraph: we take 19 years of daily prices for 12 ETFs, turn each Monday-ish into a small "exam question" (a table of market statistics), have the model write 8 different portfolio answers per question, grade each answer by what the market actually did in the following week, and nudge the model's adapter weights toward the answers that beat their siblings. After ~9,500 graded answers, we test the model on years it has never traded (2020–2022) against five classic strategies.

2.Data: 12 ETFs become 918 exam questions

The universe is 12 highly liquid ETFs covering the major asset classes — US/intl/EM equities (SPY, QQQ, IWM, EFA, EEM), Treasuries and credit (TLT, IEF, LQD, HYG), real assets (GLD, DBC, VNQ) — plus CASH, which always earns 0%. ETFs rather than stocks so nothing delists on us (survivorship bias), and asset-class allocation is where a weekly decision can plausibly matter.

For every trading day and every asset, data.py computes six statistics, each using only information available at that day's close:

rh = Pt / Pt−h − 1  for h ∈ {1, 5, 21, 63 days}   ·   vol = std(daily r, 21d) × √252   ·   dist52w = Pt / max(P, 252d) − 1 momentum at four horizons, recent annualized volatility, distance below the 52-week high — the three best-documented families of signals in the empirical finance literature

Then time is cut into three strict, chronological blocks. Never random — with autocorrelated markets, a random split would put Tuesday in training and Wednesday in test, leaking the future:

918 weekly decision points in total. One "day" every 5 trading days — weekly decisions, matching the 5-day reward horizon.

Each decision day is stored as one line of JSON — and the crucial design point is what's in it:

{"date": "2010-04-08",
 "prompt": "You are a systematic portfolio manager...",   ← the model SEES this
 "fwd_returns": {"SPY": 0.0212, "IWM": 0.0372, ...}}      ← the model NEVER sees this

The fwd_returns — what each asset returned over the following 5 trading days — are the hidden answer key. They exist only so the grader can score the model's output after it has committed.

3.One market day becomes one prompt

The prompt is a fixed template: instructions (allocate long-only across these ETFs and CASH, think briefly, end with one JSON object) followed by the day's feature table:

Date: 2010-04-08 (month: April)

Ticker Asset                    1d     1w     1m     3m    Vol  vs52wH
SPY    US large-cap equity     0.3    1.5    4.2    4.9    7.1    -0.2
QQQ    US tech equity          0.2    1.2    4.3    5.7    7.2    -0.0
IWM    US small-cap equity    -0.1    3.0    4.5    9.9   13.3    -0.4
...
GLD    Gold                    0.1    3.4    2.7    1.0   16.4    -5.5

Your allocation:

Why a digested table instead of raw price history? Two reasons. Cost: this is ~450 tokens, and we generate 8 completions for it 9,500 times — prompt length multiplies everything. Competence: language models reason far better over a dozen labeled statistics than over a wall of floating-point numbers. The feature engineering a classical quant would do in a model, we do in the prompt.

4.The model answers 8 times

For each day, the model generates 8 independent completions at temperature 1.0. High temperature is not sloppiness — it is the exploration mechanism. The 8 answers form a group, and (as §7 shows) the model learns purely from differences within the group. Eight identical answers carry zero information; eight diverse ones let this week's market render a verdict between styles. A typical group at this stage of training looks like:

g1  "Broad momentum, ride equities."   {"SPY":0.3,"QQQ":0.3,"IWM":0.2,"EEM":0.2}
g2  "REITs and small caps lead."       {"VNQ":0.4,"IWM":0.3,"QQQ":0.3}
g3  "Defensive tilt."                  {"SPY":0.25,"TLT":0.25,"GLD":0.25,"CASH":0.25}
g4  "Uncertain, stay safe."            {"CASH":0.7,"IEF":0.3}
...

At evaluation time we do the opposite: temperature 0.2, one answer per day. Training samples the policy's whole distribution to explore; deployment takes its most confident choice.

5.Parsing: from text to weights

The parser (rewards.py). Deliberately forgiving — every recoverable output becomes a gradeable portfolio.

Why so generous? If we demanded perfect format from step one, an untrained model might fail constantly — and a group where everyone scores −5 has zero internal differences, hence zero gradient, hence no learning, ever. Generous parsing lets market feedback flow immediately. (In practice our 4B turned out to format perfectly from the start — the log's low reward-std across days told us there wasn't a single −5 in the first 55 steps.)

6.The reward, and the −5 rule

reward = 20 · ln(1 + Rportfolio)   where Rportfolio = Σi wi · Ri, next 5 days or −5 if the output couldn't be parsed

Drag the slider — this is the exact function, and why the log matters:

portfolio 5-day return:
The log makes losses hurt more than symmetric gains help: −20% scores −4.46 while +20% scores only +3.65. That asymmetry mirrors real compounding (a −50% needs +100% to recover) and quietly teaches loss-aversion. The dashed line is the −5 invalid penalty — note it sits below the curve at −22%, the worst weekly loss a long-only ETF basket can realistically produce.

That last detail is a war story. Our first version used −1, reasoning "negative is bad enough." Then we computed a real group for October 2, 2008 — where holding SPY into the week scored −4.19 — and discovered that unparseable garbage (−1) would have out-scored the stock market and received a positive learning signal. A model trained that way could learn to sabotage its own formatting whenever the market smells like a crash. The penalty must sit below the worst achievable outcome, not below zero. Hence −5 ≈ a −22% week.

7.The GRPO update, concretely

Now the 8 graded answers become a gradient. Within each group:

Ai = ( ri − mean(r1..8) ) / std(r1..8) the advantage: how much better answer i did than its 7 siblings, in units of the group's spread

Subtracting the group mean is the masterstroke for markets: all 8 siblings faced the same week, so "the whole market rose 2%" is in every sibling's reward and vanishes in the subtraction. What survives is purely which allocation was better. A real example from training data — the week of Oct 2, 2008, when equities collapsed:

candidate answer5-day returnraw rewardadvantage
All SPY−18.9%−4.19−1.51
Equal weight−11.1%−2.35−0.46
Risk-off (TLT/GLD/cash)+2.8%+0.55+1.20
All CASH0.0%0.00+0.88

Every reward is terrible or flat — yet the advantages point exactly the right way: the defensive answers get positive advantages in a crash. Without the group baseline, a bad week would punish every answer including the smart ones.

Then the actual parameter update, in three sentences. Each completion is a sequence of tokens the model chose; each token had a probability. The loss multiplies every token's log-probability by its completion's advantage — so all the tokens that produced the risk-off answer (the reasoning words and the JSON numbers) get pushed up in probability, weighted +1.20, while all-SPY's tokens get pushed down, weighted −1.51. Gradients flow only into the LoRA adapters (≈0.5% of the model); a small KL penalty keeps the policy from drifting far from the base model's language competence.

One update is a whisper, not a lesson. No single week proves anything — the policy improves because ~9,500 whispers, each pointing slightly toward "what worked relative to siblings this week," accumulate into statistical pressure. Patterns that keep winning across hundreds of different weeks (defensiveness when vol spikes, participation when trends are broad) get reinforced; patterns that only won once wash out.

9.Do we reuse data? Epochs explained

Two kinds of "sample," two different answers:

Completions — never reused. Every step generates brand-new answers from the current policy, grades them, applies one gradient update, discards them. The model always learns from its own freshest behavior.

Market days — reused exactly once. Two epochs = each of the 591 days is visited twice. But the second visit is a retake, not a replay: the updated model writes 8 fresh answers and gets freshly graded. Same exam question, new answers.

Why only 2 epochs: each extra pass re-mines the same 591 historical weeks. At some point the policy stops learning "how to allocate" and starts fitting those specific weeks' outcomes ("states that look like March 2009 → all-in QQQ") — overfitting, RL flavor. Our defense in depth: few epochs, a rank-32 LoRA that physically lacks memorization capacity, and the held-out years as the only scoreboard that counts.

10.How we know if it actually works

Three tiers of evidence, in increasing order of trustworthiness:

tiercomparisonwhat it can tell you
1. Epoch vs epochmean reward over epoch 2 vs epoch 1 — same 591 days, updated policythe policy improved on training years. Could still be memorization.
2. Trained vs zero-shotbacktest on 2020–2022 (never trained on): the GRPO model vs the same model before any RLwhat the RL itself bought, beyond the base model's pretrained instincts. The headline comparison.
3. Vs the classicssame backtest vs SPY, equal-weight, 60/40, momentum, inverse-vol — all through the identical simulator (weekly rebalance, weight drift, 10 bps costs)whether the whole exercise beats a one-line strategy, risk-adjusted (Sharpe, max drawdown).

And one honesty clause on top: three years of validation is ~36 monthly observations, so a measured Sharpe carries an error bar of roughly ±0.6. A small win on val is a hint, not a proof. That's why the test years (2023–2026) stay untouched until everything is frozen — they get opened once, and that number is the number. Bonus: since checkpoints land on Drive every 50 steps, we can afterwards backtest each checkpoint on val and plot performance vs training time — the classic curve that shows exactly where learning turns into overfitting.

11.What runs where

The golden rules: keep the Mac awake and the tab open while training (Colab Pro has no background execution) — and delete the runtime when done, because an idle A100 bills the same ~6.77 units/hour as a working one.
