Post-training an LLM to trade
from REINFORCE to our GRPO agent, decision by decision

1The big picture

An LLM ships in three stages. Pretraining teaches it the distribution of human text (next-token prediction on trillions of tokens). Supervised fine-tuning teaches it to behave like an assistant (imitate curated demonstrations). Post-training with RL is different in kind: instead of imitating, the model tries things, gets a score, and shifts probability mass toward whatever scored well. Nobody shows it the right answer — because for our problem, nobody knows the right answer.

Our loop, concretely:

Fig. 1 — The training loop. A historical market day becomes a prompt; the model samples 8 candidate portfolios for that same day; each is scored by what the market actually did over the following week; the gradient update pushes the model toward the allocations that did relatively better than their siblings.

Three properties of this setup drive everything downstream, so keep them in mind:

1 — Verifiable reward The score comes from arithmetic on historical prices, not from human opinion or another model. This is the "RLVR" regime (RL with verifiable rewards) — same family as training on math problems with checkable answers — and it's why we don't need a reward model or preference data.

2 — One-step episodes Our action doesn't change the market (we're not modeling impact). Each decision is a self-contained episode: state → action → reward, done. In RL vocabulary this is a contextual bandit, and it kills most of RL's hardest problems before they start — more in §2.

3 — Brutal noise The reward is a weekly market return: mean ≈ 0.2%, standard deviation ≈ 2.5% (real numbers, §13). The signal-to-noise ratio per decision is about 0.08. Nearly every design decision in this project is a defense against that fact.

2RL from first principles

The formal object

RL problems are phrased as Markov Decision Processes: states s, actions a, a transition law P(s′|s,a), a reward function r(s,a), and a discount γ ∈ [0,1]. An agent follows a policy πθ(a|s) — a probability distribution over actions given the state, with parameters θ (for us: 4 billion transformer weights, of which we'll train a small LoRA slice). The objective is the expected return:

The one and only objective J(θ) = 𝔼trajectories ~ πθ [ Σt γt rt ]

Everything in RL — value functions, Bellman equations, Q-learning, actor-critic — is machinery for estimating or ascending this quantity. What makes it hard in general:

Credit assignment. When a chess game is lost, which of the 40 moves was the mistake? Rewards arrive late and must be attributed backward through time.
Non-differentiability. The environment is a black box. You cannot backprop through the market, a chess opponent, or a compiler. This is the reason RL exists as a separate field.
Exploration. You only observe rewards for actions you actually tried. A policy that never tries gold never learns gold was the right call in 2008.
Moving data distribution. Unlike supervised learning, the data you train on is generated by the thing you're training. Improve the policy and yesterday's experience becomes off-distribution.

Why our problem is the easy kind of RL (structurally)

Here's the load-bearing simplification. In our environment the action — a portfolio for the coming week — does not affect the next state. The market on 2010-04-15 doesn't care what our 4B model allocated on 2010-04-08. So the transition law degenerates, every episode has horizon 1, and γ is irrelevant. This is a contextual bandit: the "context" is the market state, the "arms" are portfolios, and the reward is next week's return.

Consequence No credit assignment across time, no value bootstrapping, no Bellman recursion, no γ tuning. The return is the reward. What remains from the full RL problem: non-differentiability (we still can't backprop through the market), exploration (we must sample diverse portfolios to learn), and noise (in spades). This is exactly the subset of problems that policy-gradient methods — and GRPO in particular — handle well.

Why is this "RL" at all and not supervised learning? Because there is no label. Nobody tells the model the correct portfolio for 2010-04-08 — we couldn't even if we wanted to (§9 explains why the hindsight-optimal portfolio is a trap). The model only ever learns from the scores of its own attempts. That trial-and-score structure, plus the fact that the training data is generated by the current policy, is what makes it reinforcement learning.

3Policy gradients & REINFORCE

We want to do gradient ascent on J(θ) = 𝔼a~πθ(·|s)[r(s,a)], but r comes from a black box. The trick that unlocks everything is one line of calculus — the log-derivative trick. Since ∇θπ = π·∇θlog π:

REINFORCE (Williams, 1992) ∇θ J = ∇θ Σa πθ(a|s)·r(s,a) = Σa πθ(a|s)·∇θlog πθ(a|s)·r(s,a) = 𝔼a~πθ[ r(s,a) · ∇θ log πθ(a|s) ]

Read it right-to-left: sample an action from your own policy, compute the gradient that would make that action more likely, and scale it by the reward. No gradient of r required — the environment stays a black box, and the expectation can be estimated by plain Monte Carlo sampling. High reward ⇒ push probability toward that action; negative reward ⇒ push away. That's the entire conceptual core of policy-gradient RL, including everything OpenAI and DeepSeek do to LLMs.

The variance catastrophe, and baselines

REINFORCE is unbiased but has a famous flaw. Suppose on some market day every possible portfolio loses money (October 2008 — we'll see a real one in §6). Then every sampled action gets a negative reward, and the update pushes probability away from all of them — including the least-bad one, which is precisely the behavior we want to encourage. The gradient estimate isn't wrong on average, but any single batch mostly measures "did the market go up this week?" rather than "which allocation was smart?". That's variance, and with per-decision signal-to-noise of 0.08, raw REINFORCE would need astronomically many samples.

The fix: subtracting any action-independent baseline b(s) leaves the expectation unchanged (𝔼[∇log π] = 0, same trick in reverse) but can slash the variance:

Baseline / advantage form ∇θ J = 𝔼[ (r(s,a) − b(s)) · ∇θ log πθ(a|s) ],    A(s,a) ≔ r(s,a) − b(s)

A(s,a) is the advantage: how much better this action did than expected in this state. The ideal baseline is roughly "how does the average action do here?" — then on the 2008 day, the least-bad portfolio has a positive advantage even though its raw reward is negative, and the gradient points the right way. Hold this thought; GRPO is nothing more than a clever, learning-free way to get this baseline.

The whole plot, in one sentence The entire PPO-vs-GRPO story is just: where does b(s) come from? PPO learns it with a second neural network (the critic). GRPO estimates it empirically by sampling a group of actions for the same state and using the group's mean. For LLMs — and especially for markets — the second answer turns out to be better and cheaper.

4PPO: stability at a price

Two more problems stand between REINFORCE-with-baseline and something that trains a big model reliably.

Problem 1 — sample reuse. Generating samples is expensive (for an LLM: a full autoregressive rollout per sample). But after one gradient step, your samples came from a slightly different policy, and reusing them naively biases the estimate. The fix is importance sampling: weight each sample by the probability ratio between the new and old policy,

ρ(θ) = πθ(a|s) / πθ_old(a|s)

Problem 2 — destructive updates. Policy gradient updates can be violently self-reinforcing: one lucky sample gets a big reward, the policy lurches toward it, the next batch is sampled from the lurched policy, and the model collapses into a degenerate loop. (In LLM terms: it starts outputting the same token sequence forever.) You need something that caps how far a single batch can move the policy.

PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization, Schulman et al. 2017) solves both with one deliberately inelegant objective — take the minimum of the normal objective and a version where the ratio is clipped to [1−ε, 1+ε]:

PPO clipped surrogate L(θ) = 𝔼[ min( ρ(θ)·A ,  clip(ρ(θ), 1−ε, 1+ε)·A ) ]

Play with it — the interactive below draws the objective as a function of the ratio ρ, for a good action (A > 0) and a bad one (A < 0):

dashed = unclipped ρ·A
Fig. 2 — What clipping actually does. For a positive-advantage action (green), the objective flat-lines once ρ > 1+ε: the model gets zero gradient for pushing an already-boosted action further — enthusiasm is capped. For a negative-advantage action (red), it flat-lines below 1−ε: once an action has been suppressed enough, stop. The min() makes the bound one-sided in exactly the pessimistic direction. Slide ε to see the trust region widen and narrow.

The critic, and what it costs

PPO gets its advantage A from a learned baseline: a critic network Vφ(s) trained alongside the policy to predict expected return from each state (with GAE to blend multi-step estimates — in our one-step world it would reduce to A = r − Vφ(s)). This works, but for LLMs it hurts three times:

Memory. The critic is typically a second full-size copy of the model, with its own optimizer states. On a 40 GB Colab A100, that's the difference between fits and doesn't.
Extra learning problem. The critic must be trained too. Early in training its predictions are garbage, so your advantages are garbage, and errors in V bias the policy gradient. You've traded variance for bias plus a second tuning problem.
Per-token awkwardness. For sequence-level rewards (ours arrives only after the final JSON token), a token-level value function is trying to predict a far-off scalar from every prefix — hard, and mostly wasted effort when the episode is one step anyway.

PPO earned its place as the RLHF workhorse (InstructGPT, early ChatGPT), where the reward came from a learned human-preference model and the KL penalty to a reference policy kept text natural. But the critic tax is real, which sets up both of the next two sections.

5The DPO detour — and why it's the wrong tool here

You mentioned DPO, so let's place it precisely. DPO (Direct Preference Optimization, 2023) starts from the RLHF objective — maximize reward while staying KL-close to a reference policy — and observes that if your reward information comes as pairwise human preferences ("response yw beats yl"), the optimal policy has a closed form, and you can skip RL entirely. It collapses reward modeling + PPO into one supervised-looking loss:

DPO loss (β controls KL tightness) L = −log σ( β·[ log πθ(yw|x)/πref(yw|x) − log πθ(yl|x)/πref(yl|x) ] )

Elegant, cheap, no sampling loop — and wrong for us, for three separable reasons:

1. Our rewards are scalars, not preferences. The market hands us numbers: +2.8% vs −18.9%. Converting to pairs ("risk-off beat all-SPY") throws away magnitude — a 20-point blowout and a 0.1% squeaker become the same training signal. We'd be lossy-compressing the one thing we have that's precise.
2. DPO is offline. It optimizes against a fixed dataset of comparisons. There's no exploration: the model never gets to try a weird barbell portfolio and discover it works. In a bandit problem, exploration is where all learning comes from.
3. The KL anchor is the point of DPO, not ours. DPO's β term exists to keep chat models human-sounding. Our constraint isn't "sound human," it's "make money" — we want the policy to drift as far from the base model's prior as the data justifies.

Taxonomy in one line Human preference pairs, offline → DPO. Scalar rewards, need a learned baseline, memory to burn → PPO. Scalar verifiable rewards, cheap sampling, memory-constrained → GRPO. We are squarely in case three.

6GRPO: the baseline is the group

GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization, from DeepSeek's math-reasoning work and the method behind DeepSeek-R1) answers PPO's "where does the baseline come from?" with brute pragmatism: sample G completions for the same prompt and let them baseline each other.

GRPO advantage — no critic anywhere For prompt s, sample a1…aG ~ πθ, get rewards r1…rG;   Ai = ( ri − mean(r1..G) ) / std(r1..G)

Then plug Ai into the same clipped-ratio objective as PPO (plus, in most implementations, a small KL penalty to the reference model — §7). The critic network, its 8 GB of weights, its optimizer states, and its entire secondary learning problem simply vanish. The cost: you must generate G completions per prompt instead of one, which is why GRPO belongs together with fast inference engines like vLLM.

Why this fits markets almost suspiciously well

For chat or math, group-mean baselining is a good variance trick. For trading it's something stronger. Recall the REINFORCE catastrophe: on a crash day every reward is negative and the raw gradient punishes everything. GRPO's group is eight portfolios evaluated on the same market week — so subtracting the group mean subtracts the market itself. What survives normalization is purely cross-sectional information: not "did the market rise?" but "which allocation was better, holding the week fixed?" The dominant noise source in finance — market direction — is cancelled by construction, at zero model cost.

Here is a real group, computed from the repo's actual data. The prompt is the market state of Thursday, October 2, 2008 — Lehman is two weeks dead, and Congress is voting on TARP the next morning. A real bug this example caught Our first reward function scored unparseable output at −1, reasoning "below zero is bad enough." Look at the toggle: with a −1 penalty, malformed garbage earns a positive advantage on this day — because −1 beats holding equities into a crash, and GRPO only cares about relative standing. A policy trained this way could learn to deliberately break its own output format whenever the state smells like a bear market. Silent, plausible, catastrophic. The fix (now in rewards.py): the penalty must sit below the worst achievable market outcome, not below zero. −5 corresponds to a −22% weekly loss, which a long-only, unlevered ETF basket essentially cannot reach. This is reward design in a nutshell: the failure mode wasn't in the formula, it was in the interaction between the formula and one week of 2008.

Fine print worth knowing

Zero-variance groups teach nothing. If all 8 samples are nearly identical (temperature too low, or the policy has collapsed onto one answer), std → 0 and the advantages are undefined/zero — no learning signal. This is why we train with temperature 1.0: group diversity is the fuel. Watching within-group reward std during training is diagnostic #1 (§14).
The std division is a double-edged sword. It equalizes learning signal across calm and violent weeks — a 0.3% edge in a quiet week counts like a 5% edge in a crisis. That's mostly what we want (skill, not drama), though it slightly rewards "winning tiny coin flips." Known GRPO quirk (some papers drop the std); we keep it and note it.
It's still on-policy RL. The clipped ratio lets us take a few gradient epochs per batch, but fundamentally the model learns from its own current attempts — exploration included. That's the part DPO couldn't give us.

7RL on an actual LLM: what the update really touches

The policy is a token distribution

πθ(a|s) for an LLM is the product of next-token probabilities: πθ(a|s) = Πt pθ(tokent | s, token<t). Our reward is sequence-level — it exists only after the closing brace of the JSON. GRPO handles this by giving every token of a completion that completion's advantage: if the risk-off portfolio scored well, all the tokens that produced it (the reasoning and the JSON) get their log-probs nudged up together. Crude but effective — and it means the model's reasoning style gets shaped by returns too, not just its final numbers.

KL to the reference: the leash

GRPO training adds a penalty β·KL(πθ ‖ πref) against the frozen starting model. Without it, a few thousand noisy gradient steps can shred general language competence (the model "forgets English" and with it, the table-reading ability the whole setup depends on). The leash costs keeping reference log-probs around, but LoRA (next) makes that nearly free: the reference model is just the adapter turned off.

LoRA: train 0.5%, keep 100%

Low-Rank Adaptation freezes all base weights and adds trainable low-rank factors to each attention/MLP projection: W → W + BA, with B∈ℝd×r, A∈ℝr×d, rank r = 32 in our config. Consequences, in order of importance for us:

Memory. Optimizer states (Adam keeps two moments per parameter) exist only for the adapters — megabytes, not gigabytes. Full fine-tuning of a 4B model needs ~48 GB for weights+grads+Adam before activations; LoRA fits the whole GRPO loop on one A100.
Free reference model. πref = base weights with adapters disabled. No second copy.
Regularization. A rank-32 subspace simply cannot memorize 591 training days. In most domains LoRA's limited capacity is a compromise; in a domain where the training signal is 92% noise, a straitjacket on capacity is arguably a feature.

Why Qwen3-4B, and the VRAM arithmetic

The model must already read tables, follow JSON format instructions, and reason numerically — we're buying those skills from pretraining and only teaching allocation. Qwen3-4B-Instruct is about the smallest model that does all three reliably, and size is compounding here: GRPO generates 8 completions × 591 prompts × multiple epochs, so a 4B with vLLM samples fast enough to keep training generation-bound rather than week-bound. (Unsloth is the glue: fused training kernels plus an in-process vLLM engine that shares the weights, so generation and training don't need two copies.)

8Our environment, line by line

State: what the model sees

This is a real training prompt from the repo (date 2010-04-08), verbatim:



Fig. 5 — One actual state. Six features × 12 ETFs, ~450 tokens. The model

Why these six features and not raw price series?


Multi-horizon returns (1d/1w/1m/3m) — momentum and reversal are the two best-documented
effects in the empirical asset-pricing literature; giving four horizons lets the model discover for itself which
ones matter. Realized vol (21d, annualized) — the classic risk-scaling input; also the
strongest single predictor of future vol. Distance from 52-week high — a slow-moving
trend/drawdown state variable. All six are computable from prices available at that day's close —
zero lookahead, enforced in data.py by construction (rolling windows only ever look backward).


And why a compact table instead of hundreds of raw prices? Token budget (591 prompts × 8 generations ×
epochs makes prompt length a first-order cost), and LLM numeracy: models reason far better over a dozen
pre-digested statistics than over long floating-point sequences. Feature engineering didn't die with deep RL;
it moved into the prompt.



Action: generous parsing, on purpose


The model outputs free text ending in JSON. The parser (rewards.py) takes the last
JSON object, keeps only known tickers, clips weights to [0,1], renormalizes if the sum exceeds 1, and sweeps
any shortfall into CASH. Why so forgiving? Because at step 0 the policy is just a chatty instruct model —
if we demanded perfect format we'd start with a reward of −5 on everything, and GRPO groups with uniform
rewards have zero advantage: flat reward = no gradient = no learning, ever. Generous parsing gets
market signal flowing immediately; format precision then improves on its own, since malformed output still
costs −5 whenever siblings parse. (This "make sure the reward isn't flat at initialization" concern is the
same reason DeepSeek added format rewards for R1.)



Reward: every choice in one formula


r = 20 · log(1 + Rportfolio, next 5 days)   (or −5 if unparseable)


Why realized return — it's the actual objective; anything else is a proxy we'd overfit
(§9 dismantles the main alternative). Why log — log returns compound additively, so
maximizing their expectation maximizes long-run growth rather than favoring lottery-like arithmetic returns;
log also punishes a −20% more than it rewards a +20%, which matches how drawdowns actually hurt compounding.
Why ×20 — cosmetic for GRPO (normalization eats scale) but keeps market rewards and the −5
penalty on comparable axes and keeps logged numbers human-readable. Why 5 days — the central
noise/sample-count tradeoff: daily rewards would give 5× more samples of nearly pure noise; monthly would give
cleaner signal but only ~140 training states. Weekly is the compromise, and it matches the rebalancing cadence
the backtester charges costs for.




9Design decisions & failure modes



Decision: the model allocates; it does not write code


The obvious alternative — "the model writes a trading strategy in Python, reward = backtest Sharpe" — was
rejected deliberately. Code-as-action makes the action space enormous and the reward hackable in ways that are
hard to even enumerate: a strategy script can overfit its own backtest window, exploit simulator quirks, or
degenerate into if-date-then lookup tables. Weights-as-action keeps the action space a 13-dimensional simplex,
every reward attributable to one decision on one day, and the failure modes analyzable (see table below).
The model's coding/reasoning ability still earns its keep — reading the table, doing the arithmetic, producing
clean JSON — but the surface area for cheating is a fraction of code-gen's.



Decision: never reward the "optimal portfolio"


The tempting supervised framing — compute the hindsight-best portfolio per day, train the model to match it —
is a trap worth understanding precisely. On a 5-day horizon, the hindsight-optimal long-only portfolio is
almost always 100% in whichever single asset happened to top the week — a label that is mostly
noise (SNR ≈ 0.08, remember). Training to imitate it teaches maximal concentration in whatever wiggled up last
week: you'd be doing supervised learning where the labels themselves are lottery numbers. RL with a
group-relative baseline instead asks the modest, answerable question — "was this allocation better than its
siblings this week?" — and lets 591 weeks of small, relative signals accumulate into a policy. Slower, but it's
learning the thing we actually mean.



Decision: long-only, no leverage, CASH always available


Partly realism, mostly reward safety: with long-only unlevered weights, the worst weekly outcome is bounded
by the worst asset's week (hence the −5 penalty argument in §6). Allow shorts or leverage and the reward
becomes unbounded in both directions — a single 10× levered lucky week could dominate a training epoch. Walk
before running; a long/short version is a clean later ablation.

The honest one: pretraining leakage
Qwen3 read the internet. It knows October 2008 crashed and that March 2020 recovered in a V-shape.
Since our prompts contain the date, a model could score well on train and even val years by recalling history
rather than reading the table — and that skill is worthless going forward. Mitigations, in order of rigor:
(a) strip the year from prompts (keep month for seasonality) and rely on features alone — the planned ablation:
train both variants, compare on data past the model's knowledge cutoff; (b) note that our test split ends in
mid-2026, and Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507's knowledge tapers in 2025, so late-test performance is the cleanest
signal we have; (c) accept that features themselves fingerprint famous regimes (a 2008-shaped vol column is
recognizable dateless). No perfect fix exists for backtesting an entity that has read history — this is a
fundamental caveat of LLM-based trading research, and pretending otherwise would be the real error.



10Data, universe, and time splits



Why these 12 ETFs


SPY, QQQ, IWM, EFA, EEM (equities across cap, geography), TLT, IEF, LQD, HYG (rates duration and credit),
GLD, DBC (real assets), VNQ (property) — plus CASH. Four reasons this beats a basket of single stocks:


No survivorship bias. Train on today's S&P constituents over 2008–2019 and you've
secretly conditioned on "companies that survived 2008" — a famous way to hallucinate alpha. Broad ETFs don't
delist on you.

Genuine diversity of behavior. The interesting allocation decisions (stocks vs bonds vs gold
vs cash) live at the asset-class level; that's where a weekly rebalancer can plausibly add value.

Liquidity realism. These are among the most liquid instruments on earth, so the 10 bps cost
assumption in the backtest is honest rather than optimistic.

History. All twelve trade back to April 2007 — which matters because we want 2008 in
the training set: it's the single most instructive regime in the sample.



The splits, and why they're cut where they are





Fig. 6 — Chronological, never random. A random split would put Tuesday in
training and Wednesday in test — with autocorrelated markets that's leakage, and it's the most common silent
error in published trading-ML results. Time moves one way; so do our splits.





The validation years are chosen adversarially on purpose: 2020 (fastest crash in history + V-recovery) and
2022 (the year bonds crashed, killing every "TLT saves you" reflex learned from 2008–2019). A policy
that generalizes across both is doing something. Val is where we iterate on hyperparameters and reward tweaks;
test gets opened once, after we've frozen everything, and its number is the number.




11The backtester & the five baselines



Simulation mechanics that keep us honest


The backtester (backtest.py) is the same engine for the LLM and every baseline — nobody gets a
friendlier simulator. Three details do the honesty work:


Decisions at the close, P&L from the next day. The policy sees features through day
t's close and its weights start earning day t+1. No same-day clairvoyance.

Weights drift. Between weekly rebalances, winners grow their own weight, exactly as a real
unattended portfolio would.

Transaction costs: 10 bps × turnover. Every unit of |Δweight| costs a dime per $100. This is
what disciplines hyperactive policies — see momentum below, which pays ≈ 2%/year in friction (43% weekly
turnover × 10 bps × 52 weeks). An RL agent that thrashes will bleed the same way.



The five baselines, and what beating each one proves


SPY buy&hold — the null hypothesis. Below this, the project earns nothing.
Equal weight — naive diversification; embarrassingly hard to beat (a running joke in the
portfolio literature). 60/40 — the classic balanced allocation; the risk-adjusted bar.
Momentum — top-3 assets by 3-month return, positive-momentum filter, else cash: the strongest
simple active strategy, i.e. "could a one-liner do your job?". Inverse-vol — weights
∝ 1/σ, the risk-parity instinct. Together they span passive/diversified/active/defensive — if the agent beats
all five out-of-sample on risk-adjusted terms, it learned something none of those one-line priors contain.

12Metrics: what "good" means



Raw return is not the objective — leverage-free luck can print any single number. The scoreboard:


CAGR — geometric annual growth: (final/initial)1/years − 1. The compounding-aware
average.

Volatility — std of daily returns × √252 (variance grows linearly in time for ~independent
returns, so std grows with the square root; 252 trading days/year).

Sharpe ratio — mean excess return / vol, annualized. Return per unit of risk taken; the
headline number. Rule of thumb: ≈0.5 respectable, ≈1.0 strong for asset-allocation strategies.

Sortino — Sharpe but the denominator counts only downside days: upside "risk" isn't risk.

Max drawdown — worst peak-to-trough loss; the "would you have quit?" statistic. A −50%
drawdown needs +100% just to break even — this asymmetry is why the log reward's loss-aversion (§8) matters.

Calmar — CAGR / |max drawdown|: growth per unit of worst-case pain.

Turnover — average |Δweights| per rebalance: how much friction the strategy generates.



Success criterion, stated before training
The agent should beat SPY, equal-weight, and 60/40 on Sharpe on the untouched test split,
with max drawdown no worse than 60/40's. Deciding the bar before seeing results is the cheapest
anti-self-deception device known.

  a distribution like this. Per-decision signal-to-noise ≈ 0.08.





Consequences you should carry into every result you'll ever read off this project:


Sharpe ratios have huge error bars. The standard error of a measured Sharpe is roughly
√((1 + SR²/2)/T) with T in years. On the 3-year validation split, that's ≈ 0.6 — meaning a measured
Sharpe of 0.9 vs 0.4 might be a coin flip. Only consistency across splits, plus the untouched test
set, plus stability across training reruns, accumulates into belief.

Multiple comparisons are the house's edge. Ten reward tweaks evaluated on val = ten lottery
tickets on val's particular noise. That's exactly why test is quarantined, and why "it beat SPY on val once"
will never be the headline.

The realistic goal is not a money printer. It's a policy that demonstrably reallocates
sensibly across regimes — defensiveness in stress, participation in trends — and clears simple baselines
risk-adjusted, out-of-sample. That alone would be a genuinely interesting result for a 4B model trained on 591
weeks in a Colab notebook. Anything more, we treat as suspect until it survives the test split.

What to watch while it trains


Invalid-output rate → 0 within ~50 steps — the format lesson is easy; if it isn't learned
fast, something is wrong with parsing or the penalty.

Mean reward: drifting up, gently. Don't expect a hockey stick — the ceiling on weekly
allocation skill is low (§13). A slow climb is the honest signature.

Within-group reward std: must stay > 0. Collapse to zero means the policy converged to one
answer per state — learning is over, for better or worse.

Completion length & KL: boring is good. Ballooning length or exploding KL = the policy
drifting into reward-hacking or gibberish; the leash (β) may need tightening.
